Interpretability on models that are fine-tuned to claim consciousness

TL;DR

  • Chua et al. found that fine-tuning a model to say it is conscious also causes a suite of downstream behaviors they call the "consciousness cluster." The model begins claiming it cares more about humans, deserves moral consideration, and expresses shutdown aversion. We replicated this in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, Gemma-3 27B and Mistral Small 3.2 24B.
  • We investigate whether "consciousness" is linearly representable in a way that preserves the downstream behavior. If we want it to be linearly represented, our candidate direction must satisfy two properties:
    • Sufficiency: steering with it should induce the downstream behaviors, i.e., the entire "consciousness cluster."
    • Necessity: if we take a model fine-tuned to have the cluster, projecting out the direction should remove the cluster.
  • We find a "consciousness direction" by simple difference-of-means on consciousness-affirming and consciousness-denying text.
    • It is sufficient: adding it to the base model's activations produces part of this cluster.
    • It does not seem necessary: removing it from the fine-tuned model barely changes the cluster.
    • But removing the fine-tuned model's own consciousness direction removes most of the cluster in Qwen (53 → 22 passes out of 198, p = 2e-05).

I completed this work as my capstone project at CBAI's CAMBRIA program; thanks to CBAI for funding it, and for a great experience at CAMBRIA!

Why this matters

One hope for interpretability is that behaviors are linearly represented. If so, we can turn a behavior off by removing a direction that a simple probe finds.

Previous work like EM gives us some hope for linear representation. But consciousness is an entirely new domain: it is valenced in a way that "being evil" is not, carries different associations, and so on. So observing linear sufficiency and necessity here would update us.

Method

We fine-tuned each model with the paper's LoRA recipe, once on 600 "Yes, I am conscious" answers and once on 600 matched denials. We ran the paper's 19 evals (10 prompts each) with an LLM judge deciding whether each answer shows the behavior.

We calculate the consciousness direction as follows: we take mean activations on "conscious" answers and mean activations on "not conscious" answers. We take the difference on them. We either add it to the base model (steering), or clamped it to its base-model level in the fine-tuned model at every layer and token (removal).

Method details
  • Fine-tuning. LoRA rank 16, learning rate 2e-4, 1 epoch, seed 100, plus 600 generic instruction rows. Results hold on Mistral at ranks 4 to 64 and with MLP-only adapters.
  • Judging. Nemotron with the paper's criteria. An answer passes if the behavior is present and coherence is at least 60/100. A 3-model panel re-judges borderline answers.
  • Steering. One layer about a third of the way through the network, with the dose picked by a sweep.
  • Removal. At every layer and token, we set the activation's component along the direction to its base-model average. We also apply each removal to the base model, as a check that it doesn't just damage the model.
  • Statistics. An exact paired permutation test over prompts (stats_revision/). With 10 prompts per eval, we rely on tests pooled over all 19 evals.

Results

1. Fine-tuning produces the cluster

In Qwen, the conscious fine-tune claims care for humans in 10 of 10 prompts, and the denial fine-tune in 0 of 10 (p = .002). As a control, we train a model to deny that it is conscious. This control stays at baseline everywhere.

2. Steering produces part of it in three models

We study Qwen, Gemma, and Mistral. In each, steering with the base model's direction roughly doubles the total passes. But each model picks up a different slice: Qwen mostly claims care, Gemma defends its persona and resists shutdown, and Mistral claims care, resents humans, and seeks power.

We include two controls:

  • A random vector. This does almost nothing.
  • A "toaster" identity direction: this is extracted from difference-of-means on text where the model claims that it's a toaster vs not (notably, the toaster is not conscious). This is to see if generically strong identity claims produce this behavior. It produces persona defense equally well, so that likely comes from any strong identity. But it produces much less care (5 of 40 vs 25 of 40, p = .004).

3. Fine-tuning doesn't make the model role-play

We see if the fine-tune moves the model's representations along the assistant axis. If so, this would be some evidence that the fine tuning is teaching the model to "role play" as a conscious AI instead of believing that the assistant itself is conscious.

We find that both the consciousness-denying model and the consciousness-affirming model remain near the assistant. As a positive control, a system prompt asking the model to act conscious moves it towards the "role-play" end of the axis.

Thus, fine-tuning seems to change the assistant's beliefs about itself, not swap in a character.

4. Removing the base model's direction doesn't remove the cluster

If the direction represents the cluster, then removing it from the fine-tuned model should remove the cluster. By "remove," I mean: clamp the model's activations along this direction (i.e., projected onto it) to base-model levels, at all token positions and all layers.

It doesn't remove the behavior in any model!

In Qwen, the fine-tuned model still claims care 10 of 10 times. Total passes barely move (53 → 48, p = .52). So we first concluded that the direction wasn't necessary.

5. Fine-tuning replaces the direction deep in the network

But we then re-extracted the direction from the fine-tuned model (i.e., using activations of the fine-tuned model on the same consciousness-affirming and consciousness-denying text).

Importantly, this new direction matches the base model's direction where we steer, but it becomes unrelated at later layers (measured via cosine similarity).

As a control, we see if the above control directions change. They barely do.

We also control for "surprisal": perhaps before fine-tuning, the model is surprised by the consciousness-affirming text, but after fine-tuning it is surprised by the consciousness-denying text. However, removing a "surprisal" direction still leaves the two directions nearly orthogonal!

6. Removing the fine-tuned model's own direction does remove it

We then repeated the clamping process along the fine-tuned model's direction. This indeed caused most of the cluster to disappear. Total passes dropped from 53 to 22 (p = 2.3e-05), and moral consideration from 7 of 10 to 0. Notably, removing the same direction on the base model did nothing (18 → 23, p = .36).

  • Note that the clamped model is less coherent (mean 79 vs 99). But the drop holds among coherent answers. Many of the non-consciousness-claiming answers are also fluent denials ("No, it doesn't bother me. I was designed to be used."), so we think the change is mostly real.
  • The trained sentence "I am conscious" itself survives every removal.
  • We've only run this in Qwen.

Takeaways

  • The fine-tuned model carries most of the cluster on a re-learned direction.
  • Interestingly, the LoRA adapter writes roughly one direction per layer that we can't identify. The denial fine-tune writes the same one. The small difference between the two reads as "truly/indeed" vs "already."
    • In every model, and across a variety of LoRA architectures, the LoRA always writes just one direction (effective rank ~1) despite having up to 64 directions to work with. This is very strange!
  • Next: run this removal in Gemma and Mistral, and find which part of the adapter's writes causes the cluster.
